Methods & Mathematical Reference

Recovering calcium-indicator kernels from ground-truth spike trains by regularized deconvolution

The mathematics, algorithms, and interpretation rules behind Colonel Kernel — a client-side instrument for asking whether a single linear kernel relates recorded action potentials to a measured calcium trace, and for reporting honestly when none does.

Version 1.0 Revised 2026-07-12 Status Stable reference License MIT (software)

Scope

Colonel Kernel solves the single relationship y = x ⊗ k (trace = spikes convolved with kernel) for whichever term is unknown. Its flagship problem is kernel recovery: given a known spike train and a measured calcium trace, recover the kernel k — or determine that no clean linear k exists. This is a deliberately different, better-posed problem than spike inference, and it carries a different scientific stance: the tool is a skeptic that tests whether spike–calcium coupling holds, not a believer that assumes it.

This document specifies the forward model, the recovery methods, the independent validation partner (spike-triggered averaging), the four goodness-of-fit checks, the regional-analysis rules, and the numerical conventions required to reproduce the results. It is intended to be citable by researchers who use the tool to verify ground-truth recordings.

1Data handling & privacy

Colonel Kernel runs entirely in the browser. All computation — rasterization, convolution, FFT, deconvolution, STA — is client-side; there is no backend. The application makes no third-party network requests (no CDNs, fonts, analytics, or error reporting): every library, font, and asset is bundled and served same-origin. A strict Content-Security-Policy (connect-src 'none'; default-src 'self') is injected into the shipped artifact and enforces the no-egress guarantee at the browser level; this reference page carries the same policy. Consequently a researcher’s unpublished trace data never leaves their machine, and the claim is auditable (DevTools → Network → reload → only same-origin requests). No user data is persisted beyond explicit, user-controlled file open/download.

2The problem and the three inversions

A calcium indicator turns a train of action potentials into a fluorescence signal. To first approximation, each spike contributes a stereotyped transient — a fast rise followed by an exponential decay — and the transients sum. That is exactly a linear convolution of the spike train with a single response kernel:

y(t) = ( x ⊗ k )(t) = ∑τ x(τ) k(t − τ) (1)

where x is the spike train (input), k is the kernel, and y is the calcium trace (output). This one equation admits three distinct inverse problems, and the tool exposes each as a tab:

GivenSolve forOperationRole in the tool
x, kyconvolutionTab 1 — teaching on-ramp
y, xkdeconvolutionTab 2 — flagship
y, kxdeconvolutionTab 3 — honest illustration of spike inference

Recovering k from two known signals (Tab 2) is more stable than recovering spikes x from a trace and an assumed kernel (Tab 3). Spike inference is a genuinely harder inverse problem addressed by dedicated methods (CASCADE, MLspike, OASIS; see §14); Tab 3 exists to show why naive inference struggles, not to compete with those tools.

3Signal model and definitions

Every signal in the tool obeys one in-memory contract:

Signal = { samples, dt, zeroIndex, times? }(2)
  • Spike train x. Action-potential event times (seconds), a sparse list — not pre-binned. Rasterization onto the frame grid (§4) is the tool's job.
  • Trace y. A measured ΔF/F0 time series on a dense, regular (but clock-jittered) frame grid; one column per region of interest (ROI).
  • Kernel k. The linear response of the trace to a single unit spike, expressed on a lag axis rather than on recording time.
  • dt is the nominal sample interval; times, when present, is the authoritative timebase and dt is then the derived convenience mean(diff(times)). Real fluorescence clocks jitter (±~10 µs about nominal), so a single nominal dt silently drifts spike alignment over a long recording — a correctness failure, not a rounding one.
  • zeroIndex is the 0-based sample at lag = 0. It is carried explicitly in the data structure and never re-inferred from the array center at a call site — re-deriving the origin is the classic half-sample lag bug that is invisible on a plot but inverts a coupling-direction read.

Lag convention

Recovered kernels are symmetric about lag 0: length 2·W + 1 with zeroIndex = W, where W = round(win/dt) is the half-window in samples. Negative-lag content is retained, not discarded. A causal indicator fills the positive half and sits near zero on the negative side; genuine negative-lag energy encodes lead/lag structure between cells (or a regularization artifact — §12). Retaining it turns the question from “is there a kernel?” into “is there a kernel, and what is its coupling direction?”

4The forward model (convolution)

Rasterization

Spike times are binned onto the frame grid before convolution. Two amplitude conventions matter:

  • Unit amplitude (teaching default). Each spike contributes weight 1. Colliding spikes in one bin are clamped to 1 (collisions are logged). This keeps the “is there a clean kernel?” test meaningful by forcing the kernel to explain the trace.
  • Binned count (for real data). The honest representation of real data: a bin holds the number of spikes that fall in it (the per-frame spike count). At a 10 Hz frame rate a cell can fire several times per 100 ms bin, so a bin value of 3 means three identical unit events. The count is objective — it does not introduce a free fit parameter — so it does not compromise the identifiability of Tab 2.

Timing placement is a separate axis: snap to the nearest sample (v1 default; worst-case error is half a frame, below the calcium timescale) or anti-alias across adjacent samples (planned). Placement and amplitude are the two independent arguments of a single rasterize(times, grid, method) function.

Linear, not circular

Convolution is linear (zero-padded). Circular convolution — the naive consequence of a bare FFT — is retained only as a teaching illustration of why zero-padding is necessary; it wraps the tail of the signal back onto its head and is never the analysis default.

5Kernel recovery by deconvolution

The commutativity trick

Because convolution commutes, y = x ⊗ k = k ⊗ x. A deconvolution routine that recovers the “input” from an “output” and a “point-spread function” can therefore be handed the trace as the output and the spike density as the PSF — and what it returns as the recovered input is the kernel:

k̂ = deconv( y ; x )   because   y = x ⊗ k (3)

This is the crux of the method: it recovers the unknown kernel using the known spikes, rather than attempting the harder recovery of spikes.

Regularized least squares

Deconvolution is ill-posed: naive spectral division amplifies noise without bound (the tool shows this failure deliberately). The stable formulation is a regularized least-squares problem — find the kernel whose reconvolution best matches the trace, penalized for roughness:

k̂ = argmink ‖ x ⊗ k − y ‖2  +  λ2 ‖ Lk ‖2 (4)

Here L is a smoothness operator (a discrete Laplacian — a second-difference penalty) and λ is the regularization strength. Many deconvolution routines fix this regularization at a hidden default — for example, MATLAB’s constrained-least-squares deconvolution (deconvreg) applies a Laplacian smoothness prior with no exposed parameter, so the smoothing happens silently. Colonel Kernel instead makes λ an explicit, adjustable control, because whether an apparent feature survives across λ is itself one of the four checks (§8). What was hidden becomes inspectable.

Why regularization stays visible

Negative-lag energy can be genuine cross-cell lead/lag structure or a regularization artifact (e.g. an acausal pedestal from a circular-FFT convention plus a silent Laplacian). Distinguishing the two is a human judgment, which is only possible if the regularization is exposed rather than buried in a library default.

6Three parallel recovery methods

Kernel recovery runs as three methods in parallel. None is authoritative; the spread between them is the diagnostic — when the cell is cleanly coupled they converge, and when it is not they diverge.

MethodFormWhat it contributes
Free-vector Regularized least squares, eq. (4); kernel is an unconstrained vector. The most faithful recovery; makes no shape assumption, so it can express ringing, baseline tilt, and negative-lag structure as they are.
Constrained-parametric Causal double-exponential fit (τrise, τdecay), zero baseline by construction. A deliberately minimal linear reference shape (a “foil”), analogous to STA’s cross-method role. Because it has no free baseline term it always yields a finite τdecay, even where the free-vector method cannot fit one under a baseline tilt. It fails loudly on decoupled columns — a feature.
Shape-regularized Free-vector recovery with an added baseline-flatness penalty plus a minimal low-order drift nuisance basis, combined in one objective. Protects the recovered decay from baseline drift without letting the drift basis steal the transient. The basis is a kernel-protection device, not a trustworthy drift measurement (it over-claims drift ~4×); it is held deliberately minimal.
Caveat — the foil is linear

A column that departs from the double-exponential foil may be nonlinearly coupled rather than decoupled. Deviation from the foil is not, by itself, evidence of decoupling. The parametric method is intentionally simpler than a biophysical generative model (e.g. MLspike): it omits indicator saturation and models no baseline drift.

7Spike-triggered averaging (validation partner)

Every deconvolution is read against an independent spike-triggered average (STA): the mean trace snippet in a symmetric window around each isolated spike. STA assumes no kernel and solves no inverse problem, so agreement between STA and the deconvolved kernel is a primary “is there a real kernel?” signal. It shares the deconvolved kernel’s zeroIndex origin so the two are comparable sample-for-sample about lag 0.

STA uses a different effective spike set from deconvolution — do not share one set:

  • Overlap rejection. With block = 0.5·window, an event is kept only if both neighbors are farther than block away. Deconvolution bins all spikes; STA drops overlapping ones.
  • Endpoint skip. The first and last events are skipped.
  • Per-event baseline zeroing. A pre-spike window (0.5 s) is subtracted from each event before averaging.
  • Match tolerance. Spike→sample matching uses a 0.1 s tolerance.
Read agreement alongside spike rate

STA and deconvolution have different failure modes: STA degrades at high spike frequency (windows overlap and few isolated events survive), while deconvolution holds. Their disagreement is therefore benign in the high-frequency regime but alarming in the low-frequency regime. Spike rate is surfaced as context so the reader can tell which regime applies — a late STA peak relative to the kernel is often expected behavior, not disagreement.

8The four goodness-of-fit checks

Recovery always returns something. The scientific question is whether that something is meaningful, and the tool surfaces four checks so the human can judge:

  1. Kernel plausibility. Does the recovered kernel look like a real indicator transient (fast rise, exponential decay), or like noise/ringing?
  2. Reconstruction residual. Reconvolve the known spikes with the recovered kernel and compare to the actual trace; report the residual / R2.
  3. Stability. Does the kernel stay consistent across λ settings and across spike subsets, or swing wildly?
  4. Cross-method agreement. Does the deconvolved kernel agree with the independent STA, read alongside the spike rate?

Machinery is gated; fit is reported

This is the single most easily-violated principle in the tool. Spikes and calcium can be genuinely uncoupled — that is the project’s core scientific premise, not an artifact to correct. Validation therefore splits into two things that must never be conflated:

Machinery correctnessFit quality
What Convolution arithmetic, origin/zeroIndex alignment, binned-count matching a per-frame spike histogram, output length & finiteness. The R2/residual of reconstruction against the real trace.
How treated Gated, pass/fail. Must hold regardless of coupling; verified on synthetic/known-coupled data where the expected output is known. Reported, never a global gate. A low fit on an uncoupled ROI is the correct answer, not a failure.

A correct forward path that faithfully reconstructs a coupled ROI and faithfully fails to reconstruct an uncoupled one is working exactly as intended. The spread of fit across ROIs — high on coupled cells, low on uncoupled ones — is itself the scientific signal. Consequently the tool reports mechanical facts (a parametric τ pinned to its bound; a kernel whose peak sample sits at the window edge) through a neutral indicator, and never pronounces “this fit failed” or “there is no kernel.” That verdict is the human’s; asserting it in code is the coupling claim the tool exists to leave to the scientist.

Upstream: zero spikes is a policy skip, not a poor fit

A region with zero action potentials has no input to convolve, so recovery is undefined, not poorly-fit. Such regions are skipped by policy and excluded from any decoupling-incidence denominator. A silent recording is the absence of the input the question is even asked of — it is not evidence of decoupling. (In one 72-file batch, 33 recordings were fully silent and 22 partially silent.)

What “no kernel” looks like

An uncoupled recording presents as: implausible kernel shape + high residual + unstable estimate + STA/deconvolution disagreement that the spike rate does not explain. Those four checks, together, are the “there isn’t one” verdict — which is a scientifically interesting result, not a tool failure.

9Regional analysis

A recording may span several experimental epochs (baseline, drug wash-in, high-K⁺, etc.). A kernel recovered over the whole multi-region signal is uninformative: it averages across heterogeneous epochs whose coupling may differ by design, producing a blend that is neither the baseline kernel nor any treatment kernel. The tool therefore does not render a whole-signal kernel when a recording contains more than one region; the question “is there a kernel?” is asked and answered per region. A single-region (baseline-only) recording is the boundary case where the whole and regional kernels coincide.

Analysis windows are derived from the raw region markers and the region name (names are semantic), then bracketed to the region’s spikes with a symmetric one-second buffer before recovery:

  • baseline — the interval anchored at the period end, nearest the transition.
  • treatment (any non-baseline wash-in) — a delayed start (+DELAY, default 2 min, for solution exchange) then up to a duration cap (MAX, default 20 min). This closes the wash-in AP leak: spikes still under the previous solution are not counted into the new region.
  • high-K⁺ — the entire period raw (it acts fast; no delay/cap).

Defaults (DELAY = 2 min, MIN = 12 min, MAX = 20 min) are user-adjustable. Nothing is silently dropped: sub-MIN durations are flagged but kept; only a treatment shorter than DELAY is non-analyzable. Cross-region comparison — does the kernel change across epochs? — is the scientific payoff a contaminated whole-signal average would obscure.

10The multi-ROI phenomenon

An input file carries several calcium-trace columns. The first is the default targeted cell, but it is only a default highlight — recovery runs against every trace column, with the four checks reported per column and laid side by side. This is deliberate: a recoverable kernel sometimes appears in a non-targeted ROI — a cell other than the expected one shows clean spike→trace coupling. Prevalence is unknown because the approach is not reported in the literature; running the tool over many recordings turns that unknown into a measured quantity.

Multiple-comparisons caution

Testing many ROIs against one spike train means some will fit well by chance. Before declaring a second cell “real,” compare its fit score against the distribution of scores across all ROIs (a real one stands out from the pack) and/or against a shuffled-spikes null — recovery against time-scrambled spike times, which reveals what “no real relationship” scores look like.

11Numerical conventions & reproducibility

These conventions fix how sample counts and lag alignment are computed, so results are exactly reproducible:

  • Odd, symmetric kernel. W = round(win/dt); kernel length = 2W + 1, extracted symmetrically about the center so a true lag-0 sample always exists. Recovery requires k > 2W samples of data; shorter regions are declined (“region too short”).
  • Even-length trim. When the frame count is odd, the final sample is dropped so the record has even length. This shifts only the phase alignment of the binned spike density against the frame grid; it does not affect centering, which the symmetric odd-length kernel extraction guarantees independently.
  • Edge buffer. Each region is cropped with ~1 s (10 samples at 10 Hz) of padding before the first spike and after the last, so the kernel’s edges are not data-starved.
  • Authoritative timebase. When a times vector is present it governs; a bare nominal dt is accepted with a warning that uniform-dt reconstruction can drift from the spike clock.
  • Binned-count for quantitative recovery. Kernel recovery bins spikes as per-frame counts (a spike-count histogram on the frame grid), not the unit-amplitude teaching default.
Positive control (real data)

On a real paired recording, ROI 1 yields a recoverable kernel: the recovered transient peaks at +0.6 s and holds that peak stably across λ = 0.002–3. Free-vector (+0.60 s), parametric (+0.63 s), and STA (+0.80 s) agree at the peak (the later STA peak is expected STA behavior). Yet the global reconstruction fit is imperfect (a large calcium transient near 780 s with no matching spikes; a 400–700 s stretch of reduced gain). A coupled cell with localized decoupling episodes produces a real kernel plus a less-than-perfect global fit — precisely the machinery-gated / fit-reported split, not a “no kernel” verdict.

12Limitations and caveats

  • Baseline tilt / Laplacian low-frequency blindness. The free-vector recovery can ride a slow baseline that tilts opposite the true transient, producing a whole-window correlation that is negative even when the peak agreement is strong. This is a baseline-dominated statistic and a known, still-being-characterized regularization-side artifact — not a sign inversion and not evidence against the kernel. Read peak agreement over a narrow window around the peak, not raw whole-window correlation, as the headline.
  • The parametric foil is linear. Departure from it can indicate nonlinear coupling rather than decoupling (§6).
  • Drift is modeled minimally, not measured. The shape-regularized method’s drift basis protects the kernel but over-claims the drift itself; it is not a trustworthy baseline-drift estimate.
  • Linearity assumption. The whole framework assumes a single linear kernel. Indicator saturation and other nonlinearities are outside the model; the tool’s job is to reveal when the linear assumption fails, not to repair it.
  • Not spike inference. Colonel Kernel assumes known spikes. Inferring spikes from a trace alone is a separate, harder problem and a deliberately separate concern (§14).
